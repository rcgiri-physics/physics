Newton's law of cooling

The rate of loss of heat of a body is directly proportional to the difference in temperature between the body and its surroundings, provided the temperature difference is small (about 20 °C or less) and the nature of the radiating surface remains unchanged.

If a body of mass \(m\) and specific heat \(c\) is at temperature \(T\) in surroundings at \(T_0\):

\[ -\frac{dQ}{dt} \propto (T - T_0) \quad\Rightarrow\quad -mc\,\frac{dT}{dt} = k\,(T - T_0) \] \[ \frac{dT}{dt} = -K\,(T - T_0) \] where \(K = \dfrac{k}{mc}\) is a constant for the given body and surroundings.

Solution of the differential equation

Separating the variables and integrating:

\[ \int \frac{dT}{T - T_0} = -K \int dt \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ln(T - T_0) = -Kt + C \] \[ T = T_0 + A\,e^{-Kt} \] where \(A = e^{C}\) is fixed by the initial temperature: at \(t = 0\), \(A = T_1 - T_0\).

The temperature therefore falls exponentially towards the room temperature \(T_0\), quickly at first and more slowly as the body approaches \(T_0\).

Verification (practical)

Taking logs of the solution gives a straight-line relation:

\[ \ln(T - T_0) = -Kt + \ln A \]

A graph of \(\ln(T - T_0)\) against \(t\) is a straight line with slope \(-K\). This verifies the law.

Specific heat of a liquid by the method of cooling

Two liquids of equal mass are cooled in identical calorimeters under the same conditions (same surface, same surroundings). At the same excess temperature, they lose heat at the same rate, so the liquid with the larger specific heat cools more slowly.

From Newton's law, \(\dfrac{dT}{dt} = -\dfrac{k}{mc}(T - T_0)\). For equal masses at the same \((T - T_0)\):

\[ \left|\frac{dT}{dt}\right| \propto \frac{1}{c} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{c_1}{c_2} = \frac{(dT/dt)_2}{(dT/dt)_1} \]

If both liquids cool through the same range \(\Delta\theta\) in times \(t_1\) and \(t_2\), the average rates are \(\Delta\theta/t_1\) and \(\Delta\theta/t_2\), so

\[ \frac{c_1}{c_2} = \frac{t_1}{t_2} \] The liquid that takes longer to cool has the higher specific heat.

Equal masses of water and an unknown liquid cool from 50 °C to 40 °C in identical calorimeters. Water takes 120 s and the liquid takes 90 s. Find the specific heat of the liquid. (\(c_w = 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\))

Show solution

\(\dfrac{c_l}{c_w} = \dfrac{t_l}{t_w} = \dfrac{90}{120} = \dfrac{3}{4}\)

\(c_l = \dfrac{3}{4} \times 4200 = \mathbf{3150\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}}\)

The liquid cools faster than water, so its specific heat is lower, as expected.

Do not invert the ratio. A shorter cooling time means a smaller specific heat: \(c \propto t\), not \(c \propto 1/t\).

Specific heat of a solid by the method of mixture

When a hot body is mixed with a cold one and no heat is lost to the surroundings, heat lost by the hot body = heat gained by the cold body.

Let a solid of mass \(m\) and unknown specific heat \(c\) at temperature \(T_1\) be dropped into a calorimeter containing water of mass \(m_w\) at \(T_2\). The final steady temperature is \(T\).

The water equivalent \(W\) of a calorimeter is the mass of water that would absorb the same heat as the calorimeter for the same rise in temperature: \(W = \dfrac{m_{cal}\,c_{cal}}{c_w}\) (in kg).

\[ \underbrace{m\,c\,(T_1 - T)}_{\text{heat lost by solid}} = \underbrace{(m_w + W)\,c_w\,(T - T_2)}_{\text{heat gained by water + calorimeter}} \] \[ c = \frac{(m_w + W)\,c_w\,(T - T_2)}{m\,(T_1 - T)} \]

Change of phase and latent heat

The change of a substance from one state (solid, liquid or gas) to another at constant temperature is called a change of phase.

During a change of phase:

  • the temperature stays constant;
  • the heat supplied works against the intermolecular forces (increasing potential energy), not to raise the temperature;
  • this heat is called latent heat.

The specific latent heat \(L\) of a substance is the heat required to change the phase of unit mass of it without any change in temperature. SI unit: J kg⁻¹.

\[ Q = mL \]
TypeSymbolHeat required to…Example
Latent heat of fusion\(L_f\)melt 1 kg of solid into liquid at its melting pointIce → water at 0 °C
Latent heat of vaporization\(L_v\)turn 1 kg of liquid into vapour at its boiling pointWater → steam at 100 °C

Typical values

Substance\(L_f\) (J kg⁻¹)\(L_v\) (J kg⁻¹)
Water / ice3.36 × 10⁵2.26 × 10⁶
Lead2.5 × 10⁴8.7 × 10⁵
Oxygen1.4 × 10⁴2.1 × 10⁵

Measurement of specific latent heat

(a) Latent heat of fusion of ice (method of mixture)

Dry ice at 0 °C is added to warm water (temperature \(T_1\)) in a calorimeter, and the final temperature \(T\) is noted.

  • Heat gained by ice = heat to melt it + heat to warm the melted ice from 0 °C to \(T\) = \(m_i L_f + m_i c_w T\)
  • Heat lost by water and calorimeter = \((m_w + W)\,c_w\,(T_1 - T)\)
\[ m_i L_f + m_i c_w T = (m_w + W)\,c_w\,(T_1 - T) \] \[ L_f = \frac{(m_w + W)\,c_w\,(T_1 - T) - m_i c_w T}{m_i} \]

A calorimeter of water equivalent 0.02 kg contains 0.2 kg of water at 40 °C. When 0.044 kg of ice at 0 °C is added and melts completely, the final temperature is 20 °C. Find \(L_f\). (\(c_w = 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\))

Show solution

Heat lost by water + calorimeter \(= (0.2 + 0.02) \times 4200 \times (40 - 20) = 924 \times 20 = 18\,480\ \text{J}\)

Heat gained by ice \(= 0.044\,L_f + 0.044 \times 4200 \times 20 = 0.044\,L_f + 3696\)

\(0.044\,L_f + 3696 = 18\,480 \;\Rightarrow\; 0.044\,L_f = 14\,784\)

\(L_f = \dfrac{14\,784}{0.044} = \mathbf{3.36 \times 10^5\ \text{J kg}^{-1}}\)

(b) Latent heat of vaporization of water (method of condensation)

Dry steam at 100 °C is passed into cold water (temperature \(T_1\)) in a calorimeter until the temperature rises to \(T\). The mass of steam condensed, \(m_s\), is found from the increase in mass.

  • Heat lost by steam = heat released on condensing + heat released as the condensed water cools from 100 °C to \(T\) = \(m_s L_v + m_s c_w (100 - T)\)
  • Heat gained by water and calorimeter = \((m_w + W)\,c_w\,(T - T_1)\)
\[ m_s L_v + m_s c_w (100 - T) = (m_w + W)\,c_w\,(T - T_1) \] \[ L_v = \frac{(m_w + W)\,c_w\,(T - T_1) - m_s c_w (100 - T)}{m_s} \]

0.01 kg of steam at 100 °C is passed into 0.24 kg of water at 20 °C in a copper calorimeter of mass 0.02 kg. The final temperature is 40 °C. Find \(L_v\). (\(c_w = 4200\), \(c_{Cu} = 385\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\))

Show solution

Water equivalent \(W = \dfrac{0.02 \times 385}{4200} = 0.00183\ \text{kg}\)

Heat gained \(= (0.24 + 0.00183) \times 4200 \times (40 - 20) = 1015.7 \times 20 = 20\,314\ \text{J}\)

Heat lost by steam \(= 0.01\,L_v + 0.01 \times 4200 \times (100 - 40) = 0.01\,L_v + 2520\)

\(0.01\,L_v + 2520 = 20\,314 \;\Rightarrow\; L_v = \mathbf{1.78 \times 10^6\ \text{J kg}^{-1}}\)

This is lower than the accepted \(2.26 \times 10^6\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\). In a real experiment, some heat from the steam escapes to the surroundings instead of reaching the water, so less heat appears to come from each kilogram of steam.

Always state the assumptions: no heat is lost to the surroundings, stirring gives uniform temperature, the ice is dry and at 0 °C, and the steam is dry and at 100 °C.

Exam tips

  • Newton's law of cooling: know the derivation (first-order differential equation), the exponential cooling graph, and the \(\ln(T - T_0)\) vs \(t\) verification.
  • Method of cooling: for equal masses and the same temperature fall, \(c_1/c_2 = t_1/t_2\). A common 2-mark numerical.
  • Method of mixture: the most important practical, so memorise the formula for \(c\).
  • Latent heat experiments: write the heat balance equation first. Numericals on \(L_f\) and \(L_v\) are common 4-mark questions.
  • Use the water equivalent \(W = m_{cal}c_{cal}/c_w\) when the calorimeter's material is given.
