Introduction

Wave motion is the propagation of a disturbance through a medium, transferring energy without the permanent displacement of the medium itself. Waves are classified as progressive (traveling through space) or stationary (confined to a region).

4.1 Progressive waves

A progressive wave continuously propagates through a medium, carrying energy from one location to another. Particles oscillate about equilibrium but do not move with the wave.

Wavelength \(\lambda\) is the shortest distance between two particles that oscillate in phase.

Period \(T\) is the time for one complete oscillation.

Frequency \(f = 1/T\) is the number of oscillations per second (unit: Hz).

\[ v = \lambda f = \frac{\lambda}{T} \] where \(v\) is the wave speed.

4.2 Mathematical description of wave motion

For a harmonic progressive wave traveling in the positive \(x\) direction:

\[ y(x, t) = A\sin\left( kx - \omega t + \phi \right) \] where \(A\) = amplitude, \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\) = wave number, \(\omega = 2\pi f\) = angular frequency, \(\phi\) = initial phase.

Wave speed can be expressed as

\[ v = \frac{\omega}{k} \]

Particle velocity and acceleration

The velocity and acceleration of a particle at position \(x\) are obtained by differentiating the displacement with respect to time:

\[ v_{\text{particle}} = \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = -A\omega\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi) \]
\[ a_{\text{particle}} = \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = -A\omega^2\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2 y \]

The particle velocity is not the same as wave velocity. Particle velocity is perpendicular to wave velocity in transverse waves.

4.3 Velocity of sound in different media

In fluids

The speed of longitudinal waves (sound) in a fluid depends on the bulk modulus and density:

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}} \] where \(B\) is bulk modulus (Pa), \(\rho\) is density (kg/m³).

In gases

For an ideal gas, the speed of sound is given by Newton's formula:

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}} \] where \(\gamma\) is the adiabatic index, \(P\) is pressure.

In terms of absolute temperature \(T\):

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma RT}{M}} \] where \(R = 8.314\ \text{J K}^{-1}\text{ mol}^{-1}\), \(M\) is molar mass (kg/mol).

For air at temperature \(T\) (in °C):

\[ v \approx 331 + 0.6\,T\ \text{m/s} \]

Laplace correction

Newton initially assumed isothermal compression during sound propagation, but Laplace showed that the process is adiabatic because compression and rarefaction occur rapidly, with no time for heat exchange.

Laplace's corrected formula:

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma B}{\rho}} \] where \(\gamma = C_P/C_V\) ≈ 1.4 for air.

Newton's formula underestimated sound velocity by a factor of \(\sqrt{\gamma}\) ≈ 1.18 for air.

4.4 Waves in pipes and stretched strings

Closed pipe (both ends closed or one end open, one closed)

Resonance occurs when the pipe length is an odd multiple of the quarter wavelength:

\[ L = (2n-1)\frac{\lambda}{4}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]

Fundamental frequency (first harmonic):

\[ f_1 = \frac{v}{4L} \]

Overtones (only odd harmonics):

\[ f_n = (2n-1)f_1, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]

Open pipe (both ends open)

Resonance occurs when the pipe length is an integer multiple of the half wavelength:

\[ L = n\frac{\lambda}{2}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]

Fundamental frequency:

\[ f_1 = \frac{v}{2L} \]

Harmonics (all harmonics present):

\[ f_n = nf_1, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]

Stretched string

The speed of a wave on a string depends on tension and linear mass density:

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \] where \(T\) is tension (N), \(\mu\) is linear mass density (kg/m).

For a string fixed at both ends:

\[ L = n\frac{\lambda}{2}, \quad f_n = \frac{nv}{2L} = n\frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \]

4.5 Harmonics and overtones

Fundamental or first harmonic is the lowest frequency at which a resonant object vibrates.

Overtones are any frequencies higher than the fundamental.

Harmonics are integral multiples of the fundamental frequency.

4.6 Stationary (standing) waves

A stationary wave (or standing wave) is formed by the superposition of two progressive waves of equal amplitude and frequency traveling in opposite directions.

If two waves with displacements \(y_1 = A\sin(kx - \omega t)\) and \(y_2 = A\sin(kx + \omega t)\) interfere:

\[ y = y_1 + y_2 = 2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t) \]

Nodes and antinodes

Nodes are points of zero amplitude (permanent rest) at positions where \(\sin(kx) = 0\):

\[ x_{\text{node}} = n\frac{\lambda}{2}, \quad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \]

Antinodes are points of maximum amplitude at positions where \(|\sin(kx)| = 1\):

\[ x_{\text{antinode}} = (2n+1)\frac{\lambda}{4}, \quad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \]

Worked examples

1. Wavelength and frequency. A wave travels at 20 m/s with frequency 50 Hz. Find the wavelength.

\(\lambda = v/f = 20/50 = 0.4\ \text{m}\)

2. Fundamental frequency of an open pipe. An open pipe is 0.5 m long. Speed of sound is 340 m/s. Find the fundamental frequency.

\(f_1 = v/(2L) = 340/(2 \times 0.5) = 340\ \text{Hz}\)

3. String vibration. A string of length 1 m with mass 0.01 kg and tension 100 N. Find the fundamental frequency.

\(\mu = 0.01/1 = 0.01\ \text{kg/m}\)

\(v = \sqrt{T/\mu} = \sqrt{100/0.01} = 100\ \text{m/s}\)

\(f_1 = v/(2L) = 100/2 = 50\ \text{Hz}\)

Key points

  • Wave speed: \(v = \lambda f = \omega/k\).
  • Speed of sound in fluids: \(v = \sqrt{B/\rho}\); in gases: \(v = \sqrt{\gamma RT/M}\).
  • Resonance in open pipe: \(f_n = nf_1\) (all harmonics).
  • Resonance in closed pipe: \(f_n = (2n-1)f_1\) (odd harmonics only).
  • Speed on string: \(v = \sqrt{T/\mu}\).
  • Stationary wave: no net energy transfer; amplitude varies with position.
  • Nodes and antinodes are permanently at rest and maximum oscillation, respectively.

  1. Distinguish between progressive and stationary waves.
  2. Derive the wave equation \(y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)\) for a harmonic wave.
  3. Show that the speed of sound in a gas is \(v = \sqrt{\gamma RT/M}\).
  4. An open pipe resonates at 256 Hz (fundamental). Find the next three resonant frequencies.
  5. A closed pipe resonates at 200 Hz (fundamental). Find the next two resonant frequencies.
  6. Explain why sound travels faster in solids than in gases.
  7. Show that adjacent nodes in a standing wave are separated by \(\lambda/2\).
  8. A guitar string of length 0.65 m and mass 0.003 kg is tuned to 440 Hz. Find the tension.
