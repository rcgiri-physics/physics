5.1 Work done by constant and variable force

5.1.1 Definition of work in physics

In everyday language, any physical or mental effort may be called work. In physics, however, work is defined more precisely: work is done by a force when the point of application of the force undergoes a displacement along or opposite to the line of action of the force.

If a constant force F acts on a body and the displacement d makes an angle θ with the force, then the work done is the scalar product of force and displacement:

W = F⃗ · d⃗ = Fd cos θ

Here, F is the magnitude of the force, d the magnitude of displacement, and θ the angle between the force and displacement vectors. The component of the force along the direction of displacement is F cos θ, so the work can also be written as the product of that component and the displacement.

Dimensions and units of work

  • Dimensional formula: [Work] = [Force] × [Displacement] = [M L T⁻²] × [L] = [M L² T⁻²].
  • SI unit: joule (J). One joule is the work done by a force of 1 N acting through a displacement of 1 m in the direction of the force.
  • CGS unit: erg; 1 J = 10⁷ erg.

Sign of work

  • Positive work: θ < 90°, force has a component in the direction of motion.
  • Negative work: θ > 90°, force opposes motion.
  • Zero work: θ = 90° or displacement is zero.

5.1.2 Work done by a variable force

Most real forces vary with position. For a force that changes with displacement, say F(x), we divide the motion into tiny steps dx, during each of which the force is considered nearly constant. The small work done is:

dW = F(x) dx

Thus the total work done from x₁ to x₂ is the integral:

W = ∫x₁x₂ F(x) dx

This is equal to the area under the force–displacement curve.

5.1.3 Work done in stretching or compressing a spring

For an ideal spring obeying Hooke’s law, the restoring force is:

Frestoring = −kx

To stretch the spring slowly, an external force equal and opposite to the restoring force is required:

Fext = +kx

The work done in increasing the extension from 0 to x is:

W = ∫0x kx dx = ½ kx²

5.2 Power and machine efficiency

5.2.1 Definition of power

Power is the time rate at which work is done or energy is transferred:

Pavg = ΔW / Δt
P = dW/dt

Power measures how fast work is done. A more powerful engine does the same amount of work in less time.

5.2.2 Relation between power, force and velocity

If a body moves under a force F⃗ through an infinitesimal displacement d⃗r, the elementary work is:

dW = F⃗ · d⃗r

Therefore,

P = dW/dt = F⃗ · v⃗ = Fv cos θ

Thus instantaneous power is the scalar product of force and velocity.

Units of power

  • SI unit: watt (W); 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹.
  • Practical units: kW, MW, horsepower.
  • Commercial energy unit: kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.

5.2.3 Efficiency of machines

In any real machine, some energy is lost as heat or sound. The efficiency is defined as:

η = (Useful output power / Total input power) × 100%

Equivalently,

η = (Useful output work / Total input energy) × 100%

For a water pump lifting water of mass m to height h in time t, the useful output power is mgh/t, and the required input power is:

Pin = mgh / (η t)

5.3 Work–energy theorem; kinetic and potential energy

5.3.1 Kinetic energy

Kinetic energy is the energy possessed by a body due to its motion. If a force F acts on a body of mass m and it gains speed from u to v, then:

W = ∫ F dx = ∫ m a dx = ∫ m (dv/dt) dx = ∫ m v dv

With limits from u to v,

W = ½ m v² − ½ m u²

This is the work–energy theorem:

Work done = change in kinetic energy

Hence, the kinetic energy of a body is:

Ek = ½ mv²

5.3.2 Potential energy

Potential energy is energy stored in a body or system because of its position or configuration.

Gravitational potential energy

When a body is raised to height h, the work done against gravity is stored as gravitational potential energy:

U = mgh

Elastic potential energy

For an ideal spring stretched or compressed by x, the energy stored is:

U = ½ kx²

5.4 Conservation of energy

According to the law of conservation of energy, energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only change from one form to another.

For a freely falling body, the sum of kinetic and potential energy remains constant if air resistance is neglected:

K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f

For a body falling from height h:

mgh = ½ mv²

Thus, the loss in potential energy becomes equal to the gain in kinetic energy.

A stone of mass 2 kg is dropped from a height of 10 m. Neglecting air resistance, find its speed just before reaching the ground.

Show solution

Loss in potential energy = gain in kinetic energy.

mgh = ½ mv²

v² = 2gh = 2 × 9.8 × 10 = 196

v = 14 m s⁻¹

5.5 Conservative and non-conservative forces

5.5.1 Conservative forces

A force is said to be conservative if the work done by it in moving a particle around a closed path is zero. In other words, the work depends only on the initial and final positions, not on the path followed.

  • Gravitational force
  • Elastic spring force
  • Electrostatic force

For conservative forces, a potential energy function exists.

5.5.2 Non-conservative forces

Non-conservative forces dissipate mechanical energy as heat, sound, or deformation. Examples are friction and air resistance. The work done by them depends on the path taken and cannot be recovered completely.

Wnc = ΔEmechanical

5.6 Elastic and inelastic collisions

5.6.1 Linear momentum in collisions

In the absence of external force, total linear momentum of the system remains conserved.

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

5.6.2 Elastic collision

An elastic collision is one in which both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.

½ m₁u₁² + ½ m₂u₂² = ½ m₁v₁² + ½ m₂v₂²

For one-dimensional elastic collision, the relative velocity of separation equals the relative velocity of approach:

u₁ − u₂ = v₂ − v₁

5.6.3 Inelastic collision

In an inelastic collision, momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not. Some energy is converted to heat, sound, or deformation.

For a completely inelastic collision where the bodies stick together:

v = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂) / (m₁ + m₂)

A body of mass 2 kg moving at 6 m s⁻¹ collides with a 4 kg body at rest. If they stick together, find the common final velocity.

Show solution

Using conservation of momentum:

(2 × 6) + (4 × 0) = (2 + 4) v

12 = 6v

v = 2 m s⁻¹

Summary of key results

  • Work: W = Fd cos θ
  • Variable force: W = ∫ F dx
  • Spring: W = ½ kx²
  • Power: P = dW/dt = F·v
  • Efficiency: η = (P_out / P_in) × 100%
  • Kinetic energy: E_k = ½ mv²
  • Gravitational potential energy: U = mgh
  • Elastic potential energy: U = ½ kx²
  • Conservation of mechanical energy: K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f
  • Momentum conservation in collisions: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

Exam tips

  • Always identify whether the force is constant or variable before choosing the formula.
  • Take care with the sign of work. Positive, negative and zero work all have different physical meaning.
  • Write the line of action of force and displacement clearly in vector problems.
  • In collision problems, always apply conservation of momentum first; then check whether kinetic energy is conserved.
