Introduction

A capacitor is one of the three basic passive components of electric circuits, alongside the resistor and the inductor. A resistor dissipates electrical energy as heat; a capacitor stores electrical energy in an electric field. This chapter develops the idea of capacitance, derives the capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor using Gauss's law, analyses combinations of capacitors, and examines how dielectrics change a capacitor's behaviour.

22.1 Capacitance and capacitor

The capacitor and its uses

A capacitor is a device made of two conductors, called plates, separated by an insulating medium called a dielectric. When it is connected across a potential difference, equal and opposite charges collect on the plates and an electric field is set up between them. It stores charge, and hence electrical energy.

Common uses of capacitors:

  • Energy storage: camera flashes and other rapid-discharge devices.
  • Smoothing and filtering: reducing voltage fluctuations in rectifier circuits.
  • Timing: with resistors in RC circuits for delays and pulses.
  • Tuning: variable capacitors in radio receivers.
  • Coupling and decoupling: passing AC while blocking DC in amplifiers.
  • Protection: absorbing sudden voltage surges.

Definition of capacitance

Experiments show that, for a given capacitor, the charge stored is directly proportional to the potential difference across it. The constant ratio is the capacitance.

Capacitance is the ratio of the charge on either plate to the potential difference between the plates. It measures how much charge a system stores per unit potential difference.

\[ C = \frac{Q}{V} \qquad \left(\text{or } C = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta V}\right) \] SI unit: farad, \(1\ \text{F} = 1\ \text{C V}^{-1}\).

One farad is very large. Practical values are in microfarads (1 μF = 10⁻⁶ F), nanofarads (1 nF = 10⁻⁹ F) or picofarads (1 pF = 10⁻¹² F).

Q–V graph

Since \(Q \propto V\), a graph of \(Q\) against \(V\) is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is the capacitance.

22.2 Parallel plate capacitor

Derivation of C = ε₀A/d

A parallel plate capacitor has two large, flat conducting plates of area \(A\) a small distance \(d\) apart, with vacuum or air between them. The plates carry charges \(+Q\) and \(-Q\), so the surface charge density is \(\sigma = Q/A\).

Step 1: field between the plates. By Gauss's law, a large charged sheet produces a field \(\sigma/2\varepsilon_0\) on each side. Between the plates the fields of the two oppositely charged plates are in the same direction and add; outside they cancel:

\[ E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} + \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A} \]

Step 2: potential difference. The field is uniform, so

\[ V = Ed = \frac{Qd}{\varepsilon_0 A} \]

Step 3: capacitance.

\[ C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} \] where \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{F m}^{-1}\) is the permittivity of free space, \(A\) the area of overlap of the plates and \(d\) their separation.

Effect of plate area and separation

Plate area: \(C \propto A\) (at constant \(d\)).

For the same charge, a larger area means a smaller charge density, so a weaker field and a smaller potential difference. More charge can be stored per volt.

Separation: \(C \propto 1/d\) (at constant \(A\)).

For the same charge the field \(E = Q/\varepsilon_0 A\) is unchanged, but \(V = Ed\) is smaller when the plates are closer. More charge can be stored per volt.

Effect of a dielectric

When a dielectric of relative permittivity (dielectric constant) \(\varepsilon_r\) fills the space between the plates:

\[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 A}{d} = \varepsilon_r C_0 \]

Since \(\varepsilon_r > 1\) for every dielectric, inserting a dielectric always increases the capacitance (section 22.7 explains why).

22.3 Combination of capacitors

Capacitors in series

In series, the same charge \(Q\) sits on each capacitor, and the total potential difference is the sum of the individual ones: \(V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3\).

With \(V_i = Q/C_i\):

\[ \frac{Q}{C_s} = \frac{Q}{C_1} + \frac{Q}{C_2} + \frac{Q}{C_3} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{1}{C_s} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \frac{1}{C_3} + \cdots \] The equivalent capacitance in series is always smaller than the smallest individual capacitance.

Capacitors of 4 μF and 6 μF are connected in series. Find the equivalent capacitance.

Show solution

\(\dfrac{1}{C_s} = \dfrac14 + \dfrac16 = \dfrac{5}{12}\), so \(C_s = \dfrac{12}{5} = 2.4\ \mu\text{F}\).

Capacitors in parallel

In parallel, the same potential difference \(V\) appears across each capacitor, and the total charge is the sum of the individual charges: \(Q = Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3\).

With \(Q_i = C_iV\):

\[ C_pV = C_1V + C_2V + C_3V \quad\Rightarrow\quad C_p = C_1 + C_2 + C_3 + \cdots \] The equivalent capacitance in parallel is always greater than the largest individual capacitance.

Capacitors of 5 μF and 8 μF are connected in parallel. Find the equivalent capacitance.

Show solution

\(C_p = 5 + 8 = 13\ \mu\text{F}\).

22.4 Energy stored in a charged capacitor

To charge a capacitor, work must be done in moving charge against the field between the plates. This work is stored as electrostatic potential energy.

While charging, the potential difference rises from 0 to \(V\) as the charge rises from 0 to \(Q\). Moving a small charge \(dq\) when the potential difference is \(v = q/C\) needs work \(dW = v\,dq\), so

\[ U = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C} \] \[ U = \tfrac12 QV = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C} \]

This is the area under the \(V\)–\(Q\) line, a triangle of base \(Q\) and height \(V\).

The factor ½ appears because the potential difference is not constant while charging; it grows from 0 to \(V\). The area under the graph is a triangle, not a rectangle. (The battery supplies \(QV\); the other half is dissipated as heat in the connecting resistance.)

A 10 μF capacitor is charged to 20 V. Find the energy stored.

Show solution

\(U = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \tfrac12 \times 10 \times 10^{-6} \times 20^2 = 2 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{J} = 2\ \text{mJ}\).

22.5 Loss of energy when charged capacitors are joined

When two charged capacitors are joined with like terminals together (positive to positive, negative to negative), charge flows from one to the other until they reach a common potential. The total charge is conserved, but some energy is lost, dissipated as heat in the connecting wires (and as electromagnetic radiation) by the brief current that flows.

Initial state. \(Q_1 = C_1V_1\), \(Q_2 = C_2V_2\), and

\[ U_1 = \tfrac12 C_1V_1^2 + \tfrac12 C_2V_2^2 \]

Final state. By conservation of charge, \(Q_1 + Q_2 = (C_1 + C_2)V\), so the common potential is

\[ V = \frac{C_1V_1 + C_2V_2}{C_1 + C_2} \]

and the final energy is

\[ U_2 = \tfrac12 (C_1 + C_2)V^2 = \frac{(C_1V_1 + C_2V_2)^2}{2(C_1 + C_2)} \]

Energy lost. Subtracting and simplifying:

\[ \Delta U = U_1 - U_2 = \frac{C_1C_2\,(V_1 - V_2)^2}{2(C_1 + C_2)} \]
  • \(\Delta U\) is always positive: energy is always lost, never gained.
  • It depends on the square of the difference of the initial potentials. If \(V_1 = V_2\), no charge flows and \(\Delta U = 0\).

A 4 μF capacitor charged to 100 V is connected to a 2 μF capacitor charged to 40 V, positive terminals together. Find (i) the common potential, (ii) the initial energy, (iii) the final energy, (iv) the energy lost.

Show solution

(i) \(V = \dfrac{4 \times 100 + 2 \times 40}{4 + 2} = \dfrac{480}{6} = 80\ \text{V}\)

(ii) \(U_1 = \tfrac12 (4 \times 10^{-6})(100)^2 + \tfrac12 (2 \times 10^{-6})(40)^2 = 0.0200 + 0.0016 = 21.6\ \text{mJ}\)

(iii) \(U_2 = \tfrac12 (6 \times 10^{-6})(80)^2 = 19.2\ \text{mJ}\)

(iv) \(\Delta U = 21.6 - 19.2 = 2.4\ \text{mJ}\)

Check: \(\Delta U = \dfrac{(4 \times 10^{-6})(2 \times 10^{-6})(60)^2}{2 \times 6 \times 10^{-6}} = 2.4 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{J}\) ✓

22.6 Common potential

When several capacitors charged to different potentials are joined in parallel (like terminals together), conservation of charge gives the common potential:

\[ V = \frac{C_1V_1 + C_2V_2 + C_3V_3 + \cdots}{C_1 + C_2 + C_3 + \cdots} = \frac{\sum C_iV_i}{\sum C_i} \]
  • The common potential is a weighted average of the initial potentials, weighted by capacitance; larger capacitors have more influence.
  • It always lies between the highest and lowest initial potentials.
  • Charge flows from the capacitors at higher potential to those at lower potential until all are at the same potential.

Identical capacitors. If all capacitances are equal, \(V = \dfrac{V_1 + V_2 + \cdots + V_n}{n}\), the arithmetic mean.

One much larger. If \(C_1 \gg C_2\), then \(V \approx V_1\): the large capacitor fixes the potential.

Capacitors \(C_1 = 3\) μF at 60 V, \(C_2 = 2\) μF at 40 V and \(C_3 = 5\) μF at 20 V are joined in parallel (like terminals together). Find the common potential and the final charge on each.

Show solution

\(V = \dfrac{3 \times 60 + 2 \times 40 + 5 \times 20}{3 + 2 + 5} = \dfrac{360}{10} = 36\ \text{V}\)

Total charge before: \(180 + 80 + 100 = 360\ \mu\text{C}\); after: \(10 \times 36 = 360\ \mu\text{C}\) ✓

Final charges: \(Q_1' = 3 \times 36 = 108\ \mu\text{C}\), \(Q_2' = 2 \times 36 = 72\ \mu\text{C}\), \(Q_3' = 5 \times 36 = 180\ \mu\text{C}\).

22.7 Effect of a dielectric

Polarization

A dielectric is an electrical insulator. In an electric field it becomes polarized: the positive and negative charges in each molecule shift slightly in opposite directions (or permanent molecular dipoles line up), producing dipoles aligned with the field.

Inserting a dielectric into an isolated capacitor

Bound charges appear on the faces of the dielectric next to the plates. They produce an induced field opposing the original field, so the net field between the plates is reduced by the factor \(\varepsilon_r\). For a charged capacitor that is isolated (disconnected from the battery):

\[ Q = \text{constant}, \qquad E = \frac{E_0}{\varepsilon_r}, \qquad V = \frac{V_0}{\varepsilon_r}, \qquad C = \frac{Q}{V} = \varepsilon_r C_0 \] \[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 A}{d} \]

Know which quantity stays fixed. Isolated capacitor: \(Q\) constant, so \(V\), \(E\) and \(U = Q^2/2C\) all fall by \(\varepsilon_r\). Still connected to the battery: \(V\) constant, so \(Q\) and \(U = \tfrac12 CV^2\) rise by \(\varepsilon_r\), and \(E = V/d\) is unchanged.

Key formulas

  • \(C = Q/V\); parallel plates: \(C = \varepsilon_0 A/d\) (vacuum), \(C = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0 A/d\) (dielectric).
  • Series: \(1/C_s = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2 + \cdots\) (same \(Q\)); parallel: \(C_p = C_1 + C_2 + \cdots\) (same \(V\)).
  • Energy: \(U = \tfrac12 QV = \tfrac12 CV^2 = Q^2/2C\).
  • Common potential: \(V = \sum C_iV_i / \sum C_i\).
  • Energy lost on joining: \(\Delta U = \dfrac{C_1C_2(V_1 - V_2)^2}{2(C_1 + C_2)}\).
