Chapter 8: Elasticity

Class: Grade XI  |  Board: National Examination Board (NEB), Nepal

Introduction

A rigid body is an idealisation. In reality, every solid changes its shape or size, however slightly, when forces are applied to it. A steel wire lengthens when a load is hung from it, a rubber band stretches, and a spring compresses. This ability of solids to resist deformation and recover their original form when the applied force is removed is called elasticity. In this chapter we study the relation between force and deformation, stress and strain, elastic moduli, and the energy stored in deformed bodies.

8.1 Hooke's Law: Force Constant

8.1.1 Deforming Force and Restoring Force

A deforming force is an external force that changes the length, volume or shape of a body. When a body is deformed, the atoms or molecules of which it is made are displaced from their equilibrium positions. The body develops internal restoring forces that oppose the deformation.

8.1.2 Statement of Hooke's Law

Robert Hooke (1678) found experimentally that, for small deformations, the deformation produced is directly proportional to the deforming force.

Hooke's law: Within the elastic limit, the extension (or compression) produced in a body is directly proportional to the deforming force applied.

For a spring or a wire, if a force F produces an extension x, then

F = kx

where k is a constant of proportionality called the force constant (or spring constant). The restoring force exerted by the spring on the agent that stretches it has the same magnitude but opposite direction. Hence

Frestoring = −kx

The negative sign indicates that the restoring force is always directed opposite to the displacement x measured from the natural (unstretched) length.

8.1.3 Force Constant

The force constant k is defined as the force required per unit extension:

k = F / x

Its SI unit is newton per metre (N m−1) and its dimensional formula is [M L0 T−2]. A large value of k means a stiff spring, which needs a large force to produce a small extension.

   Force F
     |                     /
     |                    /  <-- straight line: F = kx
     |                   /
     |                  /
     |                 /
     |                /
     |               /
     |              /   slope of the line = k
     |             /
     +------------/----------------- Extension x
     0
     (valid only up to the proportional / elastic limit)

The slope of the force–extension graph gives the force constant k.

8.1.4 Force Constant of a Wire

A wire of natural length L and cross-sectional area A, made of a material of Young's modulus Y (defined in Section 8.3), obeys Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L). Rearranging,

F = (YA/L) ΔL, and comparing with F = kx (with x = ΔL):

k = YA / L

Thus the force constant of a wire is directly proportional to its cross-sectional area and inversely proportional to its length. A thicker wire is stiffer; a longer wire is softer.

8.1.5 Combination of Springs

(a) Springs in series. Two springs of force constants k1 and k2 are joined end to end and a force F is applied. Each spring carries the same force F, so the extensions add:

F/ks = F/k1 + F/k2

1/ks = 1/k1 + 1/k2, i.e. ks = k1k2/(k1 + k2)

(b) Springs in parallel. Two springs are connected side by side so that both are stretched by the same extension x. The forces add: F = F1 + F2 = k1x + k2x.

kp = k1 + k2

(c) Cutting a spring. A spring of force constant k, cut into n equal parts, gives parts each of force constant nk. Reason: the original spring is equivalent to n identical pieces in series, so the effective constant becomes k/n? Wait: for a spring of length L, the force constant is inversely proportional to length. Hence each half has twice the constant; each one-third has triple the constant. For n equal parts, the constant of each part is nk.

8.1.6 Limits of Hooke's Law

Hooke's law is not a universal law of nature but an approximation valid for small deformations. Beyond a certain value of the deforming force, called the proportional limit, the extension is no longer proportional to the force. If the material is loaded beyond the elastic limit, it may not return fully to its original shape after the force is removed.

Common Misconception

The force constant k is not the same as Young's modulus Y. Y is a property of the material only; k also depends on the length and cross-section of the particular specimen (k = YA/L for a wire).

Real-Life Applications

  • Spring balances measure weight because the extension of the spring is proportional to the load.
  • Vehicle suspension springs and shock absorbers are chosen with a suitable k for the load they must carry.
  • Mechanical watches, weighing scales and force gauges all rely on linear elastic response.

Worked Example 1 (Easy – Concept Clarity)

Problem: A force of 10 N stretches a spring by 5 cm. Find (a) the force constant, (b) the force needed to stretch the same spring by 8 cm.

Solution:

(a) k = F/x = 10 N / 0.05 m = 200 N m−1

(b) F = kx = 200 × 0.08 = 16 N

Answer: k = 200 N m−1; F = 16 N

Worked Example 2 (Moderate – Practice Level)

Problem: Two springs of force constants 200 N m−1 and 300 N m−1 are joined (a) in parallel, (b) in series. Find the equivalent force constant in each case.

Solution:

(a) Parallel: kp = 200 + 300 = 500 N m−1. If a force of 30 N is applied, extension x = F/kp = 30/500 = 0.06 m = 6 cm.

(b) Series: ks = (200 × 300)/(200 + 300) = 60000/500 = 120 N m−1. Extension x = F/ks = 30/120 = 0.25 m = 25 cm.

Answer: kp = 500 N m−1, x = 6 cm; ks = 120 N m−1, x = 25 cm

Worked Example 3 (Exam-Level – NEB Pattern)

Problem: A spring of force constant 600 N m−1 is cut into three equal parts. Two of the parts are connected in parallel and a mass of 6 kg is hung from the combination.

Solution:

Each part has force constant k′ = nk = 3 × 600 = 1800 N m−1.

Two parts in parallel: kp = 1800 + 1800 = 3600 N m−1.

Load F = mg = 6 × 9.8 = 58.8 N.

Extension x = F/kp = 58.8/3600 = 0.01633 m

Answer: k′ = 1800 N m−1; x ≈ 1.63 cm

8.2 Stress, Strain, Elasticity and Plasticity

8.2.1 Elasticity and Plasticity

Elasticity is the property of a material by virtue of which it tends to regain its original size and shape when the deforming force is removed. A body that completely regains its original form is said to be perfectly elastic.

Plasticity is the property by which a material does not regain its original size and shape on removal of the deforming force, but retains, wholly or partly, the deformation. A body that does not regain its shape is called plastic.

8.2.2 Stress

When a body is deformed, internal restoring forces develop. Stress is defined as the restoring force developed per unit area of cross-section of the deformed body. At equilibrium, it equals the applied force divided by the area.

Stress = F / A

SI unit: N m−2 or pascal (Pa). Dimensional formula: [M L−1 T−2]. Although stress is force per unit area, it is a tensor quantity, not a vector quantity.

  • Longitudinal (normal) stress: the force acts perpendicular to the area. If it tends to lengthen the body it is tensile stress; if it tends to shorten it, compressive stress.
  • Volume (bulk) stress: a normal force acts uniformly over the entire surface of the body, as when a body is immersed in a fluid. The volume stress equals the increase in pressure ΔP applied.
  • Shear (tangential) stress: the force acts tangential to the surface. Shear stress = Ft/A, where Ft is the tangential force and A is the area of the face on which it acts.

8.2.3 Strain

Strain is the fractional deformation produced in a body under stress. It is a ratio of two like quantities, hence dimensionless and unitless.

  • Longitudinal strain = change in length / original length = ΔL / L
  • Volume strain = change in volume / original volume = ΔV / V
  • Shear strain = angle θ through which a face is displaced (in radians) = Δx / h, where Δx is the relative displacement of the two faces and h is the distance between them.
 (a) Longitudinal strain          (b) Volume strain          (c) Shear strain

  fixed                                  ______                    F
  |=========|====> F                   |      |                 ------>
  |<--  L  -->|<>                    |      |  <-- P          _________        _________
              ΔL                      |______|                 |         |      /         /
                                      (uniform pressure P         |         |  h  /         /
  strain = ΔL / L                   on all faces)             |_________|    /_________/
                                      strain = ΔV / V          (fixed base)      <-Δx->
                                                                  strain = tanθ = Δx / h

8.2.4 Stress–Strain Curve

When a wire is loaded gradually and the stress is plotted against the resulting strain, a characteristic curve is obtained.

  Stress
    |                          D
    |                      .-'''-.
    |                  .-''        '-.
    |               C .'               '. E  (fracture)
    |              .-'                    *
    |           B *
    |         A *
    |         /
    |       /            OA : straight line (Hooke's law)
    |     /              A  : proportional limit
    |   /                B  : elastic limit
    | /                  C  : yield point
    +--------------------------------------- Strain
    O                    D  : ultimate tensile strength
                         E  : fracture (breaking) point
  • O to A (proportional region): stress is proportional to strain. Hooke's law holds. The slope is Young's modulus. A is the proportional limit.
  • A to B: the wire is still elastic (returns to original length on unloading) but stress is no longer proportional to strain. B is the elastic limit: the maximum stress up to which the material regains its original form.
  • B to C: beyond the elastic limit, permanent (plastic) deformation sets in. At the yield point C, the strain increases rapidly with little or no increase in stress.
  • C to D: strain hardening; more stress is required for further strain. D corresponds to the ultimate tensile strength, the maximum stress the material can withstand.
  • D to E: the wire develops a local narrowing (necking), the stress falls, and the wire breaks at E. The stress at which the wire breaks is the breaking stress.

For many materials the proportional limit and elastic limit are very close together, and they are often not distinguished at school level. Strictly, they are different points.

8.2.5 Ductile and Brittle Materials; Some Related Terms

  • Ductile materials (copper, mild steel, aluminium) show a large plastic region between the elastic limit and the breaking point, and can be drawn into wires.
  • Brittle materials (glass, cast iron, ceramics) break soon after the elastic limit with very little plastic deformation.
  • Elastic hysteresis: when a material is loaded and then unloaded, the stress–strain curves for loading and unloading may not coincide (this is very pronounced in rubber). The area enclosed represents energy lost as heat.
  • Elastic fatigue: the loss of strength of a material after it has been subjected to repeated cycles of stress. Bridges, aircraft parts and machine components fail by fatigue after many cycles.
  • Factor of safety: the ratio of the breaking stress to the maximum working stress allowed in a design. Structures are built with a factor of safety greater than 1 to allow for uncertainties.

Common Misconceptions

  • A material that stretches more is not more elastic. Steel is more elastic than rubber in the physics sense because, for the same stress, steel shows a much smaller strain and regains its shape more completely.
  • Stress is not the same as pressure, although both have units of N m−2. Pressure is a normal force per unit area exerted by a fluid, whereas stress is an internal restoring force per unit area within a solid.
  • Strain is dimensionless. Never attach a unit to it.

Real-Life Applications

  • Crane ropes are made by twisting many thin wires together. A thin wire is more flexible and, for a given metal, has a high breaking stress per unit area than a thick rod.
  • Bridge beams and building girders are given an I-shaped cross-section to resist bending with minimum material.
  • Engineers apply a factor of safety in designing lifts, cables and pressure vessels.

Worked Example 4 (Easy – Concept Clarity)

Problem: A steel wire of cross-sectional area 2 mm2 and length 2 m is stretched by a force of 100 N and its length increases by 0.5 mm. Find the tensile stress, the longitudinal strain and Young's modulus.

Solution:

A = 2 mm2 = 2 × 10−6 m2; ΔL = 0.5 mm = 5 × 10−4 m.

Stress = F/A = 100 / (2 × 10−6) = 5 × 107 N m−2

Strain = ΔL/L = (5 × 10−4) / 2 = 2.5 × 10−4

Y = stress / strain = (5 × 107) / (2.5 × 10−4) = 2 × 1011 N m−2

Answer: stress = 5 × 107 Pa; strain = 2.5 × 10−4; Y = 2 × 1011 Pa

8.3 Elastic Modulus: Young Modulus, Bulk Modulus, Shear Modulus

8.3.1 Elastic Modulus

Within the limit of proportionality, Hooke's law can be restated: stress is proportional to strain. The constant of proportionality is called the modulus of elasticity (elastic modulus).

Elastic modulus = stress / strain

Since strain is dimensionless, the elastic modulus has the same unit and dimensions as stress: pascal (N m−2), [M L−1 T−2]. It depends only on the material, not on the size or shape of the specimen.

8.3.2 Young Modulus (Y)

The ratio of longitudinal (tensile or compressive) stress to longitudinal strain is the Young modulus.

Y = (F/A) / (ΔL/L) = FL / (AΔL)

For a wire of circular cross-section of diameter d, A = πd2/4, so Y = 4FL / (πd2ΔL).

Physical meaning: Y measures the stiffness of a material against stretching or compression. A larger Y means a larger stress is needed to produce a given strain. Y is defined for solids only.

Elongation of a Rod under Its Own Weight (Extension)

Assumptions: a uniform rod of length L, cross-sectional area A, density ρ and Young modulus Y hangs vertically from a rigid support; strains are small.

Consider a small element of length dy at a distance y from the lower (free) end. The tension in the rod at this section supports the weight of the rod below it: T(y) = ρAyg. The element's extension is

d(ΔL) = T(y) dy / (AY) = ρAyg dy / (AY) = (ρg/Y) y dy

Total extension:

ΔL = (ρg/Y) ∫0L y dy = (ρg/Y)(L2/2)

ΔL = ρgL2 / 2Y = MgL / 2AY  (where M = ρAL is the mass of the rod)

This is half the extension that would result if the whole weight Mg were hung at the free end, because the tension decreases uniformly from Mg at the top to zero at the bottom.

8.3.3 Bulk Modulus (K)

When a uniform pressure change ΔP is applied over the whole surface of a body, its volume changes by ΔV. The bulk modulus is the ratio of volume stress to volume strain:

K = − ΔP / (ΔV/V) = − V ΔP / ΔV

The negative sign is included because an increase in pressure (ΔP > 0) decreases the volume (ΔV < 0), and K is defined to be a positive quantity. The reciprocal of the bulk modulus is the compressibility.

Physical meaning: K measures resistance to uniform compression. It is defined for solids, liquids and gases. Liquids and solids have large K (they are nearly incompressible); gases have small K.

8.3.4 Shear Modulus (η), also called Modulus of Rigidity

When a tangential force F is applied to the top face of area A of a body whose bottom face is fixed, the body is sheared through an angle θ. The shear modulus is the ratio of shear stress to shear strain:

η = (F/A) / θ = F / (Aθ)

where θ = Δx/h (in radians, for small angles). Physical meaning: η measures resistance to change of shape at constant volume. It is meaningful only for solids, since liquids and gases cannot sustain shear stress.

8.3.5 Approximate Values of Elastic Moduli

  • Steel: Y ≈ 2.0 × 1011 Pa; K ≈ 1.6 × 1011 Pa; η ≈ 8 × 1010 Pa
  • Copper: Y ≈ 1.1 × 1011 Pa; η ≈ 4 × 1010 Pa
  • Aluminium: Y ≈ 7 × 1010 Pa; η ≈ 2.5 × 1010 Pa
  • Water: K ≈ 2.2 × 109 Pa; η = 0
  • Rubber: Y of the order of 106 to 107 Pa

These are typical values; the exact figures vary slightly between reference books. In examination problems, use the values given in the question.

Common Misconceptions

  • Young modulus, bulk modulus and shear modulus are all constants of the material, unlike the force constant k, which depends on the specimen.
  • Bulk modulus is the only one of the three defined for liquids and gases. Young modulus and shear modulus apply to solids only.
  • The larger the elastic modulus, the less deformable (the "stiffer") the material, not the more stretchable.

Real-Life Applications

  • The Young modulus governs the choice of material for cables, columns and beams.
  • The bulk modulus of a medium determines the speed of sound in it (v = √(K/ρ) in a fluid), and the compressibility of water is important for the design of submarines and deep-sea equipment.
  • The shear modulus governs the twisting of shafts and the design of rubber mounts and rivets.

Worked Example 5 (Easy – Concept Clarity)

Problem: The pressure on a sample of water is increased by 2 × 107 Pa. If the bulk modulus of water is 2.2 × 109 Pa, find the fractional decrease in its volume.

Solution:

K = −ΔP/(ΔV/V), so |ΔV/V| = ΔP/K = (2 × 107)/(2.2 × 109) = 9.09 × 10−3

Answer: ΔV/V ≈ 9.1 × 10−3 (a decrease of about 0.91%)

Worked Example 6 (Moderate – Practice Level)

Problem: The top face of a cube of side 10 cm made of a material of shear modulus 2 × 1010 Pa is acted upon by a tangential force of 5 × 103 N, while the base is fixed. Find the angle of shear and the relative displacement of the top face.

Solution:

Area of face A = 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01 m2; h = 0.1 m.

Shear stress = F/A = 5 × 103/0.01 = 5 × 105 Pa

θ = stress/η = (5 × 105)/(2 × 1010) = 2.5 × 10−5 rad

Δx = θh = 2.5 × 10−5 × 0.1 = 2.5 × 10−6 m

Answer: stress = 5 × 105 Pa; θ = 2.5 × 10−5 rad; Δx = 2.5 µm

Worked Example 7 (Exam-Level – NEB Pattern)

Problem: A steel wire of length 2 m and diameter 1 mm supports a mass of 10 kg. Calculate (a) the stress, (b) the strain, (c) the extension. (Y = 2 × 1011 Pa, g = 9.8 m s−2)

Solution:

F = mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N

A = πd2/4 = π(1 × 10−3)2/4 = 7.854 × 10−7 m2

(a) Stress = F/A = 98/(7.854 × 10−7) = 1.248 × 108 Pa

(b) Strain = stress/Y = (1.248 × 108)/(2 × 1011) = 6.24 × 10−4

(c) ΔL = strain × L = 6.24 × 10−4 × 2 = 1.248 × 10−3 m

Answer: stress ≈ 1.25 × 108 Pa; strain ≈ 6.24 × 10−4; extension ≈ 1.25 mm

Worked Example 8 (Exam-Level – Ratio Problem)

Problem: Two wires A and B of the same material are subjected to the same load. The lengths are in the ratio 1 : 2 and the diameters in the ratio 2 : 1. Find the ratio of their extensions.

Solution: From Y = FL/(AΔL), ΔL = FL/(AY). With F and Y the same and A ∝ d2, ΔL ∝ L/d2.

ΔLA/ΔLB = (LA/LB) × (dB/dA)2 = (1/2) × (1/2)2 = 1/8

Answer: ΔLA : ΔLB = 1 : 8

Worked Example 9 (Exam-Level – Own Weight)

Problem: A vertical steel rod 100 m long hangs from a rigid support. Find its extension due to its own weight. (ρ = 7800 kg m−3, Y = 2 × 1011 Pa, g = 9.8 m s−2)

Solution:

ΔL = ρgL2/2Y = (7800 × 9.8 × 1002)/(2 × 2 × 1011) = (7.644 × 108)/(4 × 1011) = 1.91 × 10−3 m

Answer: ΔL ≈ 1.9 mm

8.4 Poisson's Ratio

8.4.1 Lateral Strain and Poisson's Ratio

When a wire or bar is stretched along its length, it becomes thinner; when it is compressed, it becomes thicker. The strain produced perpendicular to the applied force is called lateral strain.

      before                       after tension
     +-------+                      +-----+
     |       |                      |     |
     |       | d                    |     |  d - Δd   (contracts)
     |       |                      |     |
     |       |  L                   |     |  L + ΔL   (extends)
     |       |                      |     |
     +-------+                      |     |
                                     +-----+

If a bar of original diameter d and length L is stretched so that its length increases by ΔL and its diameter decreases by Δd, then

  • Longitudinal strain = ΔL / L
  • Lateral strain = −Δd / d (negative when the diameter decreases as the length increases)

Poisson's ratio σ is defined as the ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain, taken with a sign so that the ratio is normally positive:

σ = − (lateral strain) / (longitudinal strain) = (Δd / d) / (ΔL / L)   (with Δd taken as the magnitude of the contraction)

Poisson's ratio is dimensionless and has no unit.

Physical meaning: σ tells how much a material narrows for a given lengthening. A material with σ = 0.3, stretched by 1%, contracts laterally by 0.3%.

8.4.2 Limits of Poisson's Ratio

Volume change under tension. Assumptions: a cylindrical wire, small strains, isotropic material. The volume is V = πr2L. Taking natural logarithms,

ΔV / V = (1 − 2σ) ΔL / L

A body stretched by a tensile force cannot decrease in volume, so ΔV/V ≥ 0, which requires 1 − 2σ ≥ 0, i.e. σ ≤ 0.5. The value σ = 0.5 corresponds to a perfectly incompressible material.

8.4.3 Relations Among Y, K, η and σ

(a) Relation between Y, K and σ. Assumption: a cube of an isotropic material is subjected to a uniform pressure P on all six faces.

Consider the strain along one edge. The compressive stress P along that edge alone produces a compressive strain P/Y. The stresses P applied along the other two perpendicular directions each cause lateral strain σP/Y. Hence the net linear strain is

ΔL/L = −P/Y + 2σP/Y = −(P/Y)(1 − 2σ)

For small strains, the volume strain is three times the linear strain (V = L3, so dV/V = 3 dL/L):

ΔV/V = −3(P/Y)(1 − 2σ)

By definition K = −P/(ΔV/V). Therefore

K = Y / 3(1 − 2σ)   or   Y = 3K(1 − 2σ)

Since K must be positive, this again requires σ < 0.5.

(b) Relation between Y, η and σ. A pure shear stress τ is equivalent to a tensile stress +τ along one diagonal of the element together with a compressive stress −τ along the other diagonal. This gives

Y = 2η(1 + σ)

Since η > 0, this requires σ > −1, which is the lower limit stated above.

(c) Eliminating σ. From the two relations, 1 − 2σ = Y/3K and 2(1 + σ) = Y/η. Adding gives 3 = Y/3K + Y/η, so

9/Y = 3/η + 1/K

which is useful when any two of the three moduli are given and the third is required.

Common Misconception

Poisson's ratio is not the ratio of the change in diameter to the change in length. It is the ratio of the fractional (relative) changes, Δd/d divided by ΔL/L.

Real-Life Applications

  • Rubber gaskets and seals rely on σ ≈ 0.5: squeezing them in one direction makes them bulge sideways and fill gaps.
  • Cork stoppers (σ ≈ 0) can be pushed into a bottle neck without bulging sideways.
  • Engineers use σ to compute the change in cross-section of cables and columns under load.

Worked Example 10 (Easy – Concept Clarity)

Problem: A wire 2 m long and 2 mm in diameter is stretched so that its length increases by 1 mm. If Poisson's ratio of the material is 0.30, find the decrease in diameter.

Solution:

Longitudinal strain = 1 mm / 2000 mm = 5 × 10−4

Lateral strain = σ × longitudinal strain = 0.30 × 5 × 10−4 = 1.5 × 10−4

Δd = lateral strain × d = 1.5 × 10−4 × 2 mm = 3 × 10−4 mm = 3 × 10−7 m

Answer: the diameter decreases by 3 × 10−7 m

Worked Example 11 (Moderate – Practice Level)

Problem: For the wire in Example 10, find the fractional change in its volume.

Solution:

ΔV/V = (1 − 2σ) ΔL/L = (1 − 0.60) × 5 × 10−4 = 0.40 × 5 × 10−4 = 2 × 10−4

Answer: ΔV/V = 2 × 10−4 (volume increases by 0.02%)

Worked Example 12 (Exam-Level – NEB Pattern)

Problem: For a metal, Young's modulus is 2.0 × 1011 Pa and bulk modulus is 1.67 × 1011 Pa. Find Poisson's ratio and the shear modulus.

Solution:

From Y = 3K(1 − 2σ): 1 − 2σ = Y/3K = (2.0 × 1011)/(3 × 1.67 × 1011) = 0.399

σ = (1 − 0.399)/2 = 0.30

From Y = 2η(1 + σ): η = Y/[2(1 + σ)] = (2.0 × 1011)/(2 × 1.30) = 7.7 × 1010 Pa

Check: 9/Y = 4.5 × 10−11 and 3/η + 1/K = 3.9 × 10−11 + 0.6 × 10−11 = 4.5 × 10−11 (Pa−1)

Answer: σ ≈ 0.30; η ≈ 7.7 × 1010 Pa

8.5 Elastic Potential Energy

8.5.1 Concept

To deform an elastic body, work must be done against the internal restoring forces. If the body is deformed within its elastic limit and the deformation is carried out slowly, this work is stored as elastic potential energy.

8.5.2 Elastic Potential Energy of a Stretched Spring

Assumptions: the spring obeys Hooke's law throughout; the stretching is done slowly so that kinetic energy is negligible.

When the extension is x, the applied force is F = kx. The work done in producing a further small extension dx is

dW = F dx = kx dx

The total work done in stretching the spring from zero to an extension x is

W = ∫0x kx dx = k [x2/2]0x

U = ½ k x2 = ½ F x

Because the force increases linearly from 0 to F, the work done equals the average force (F/2) multiplied by the extension.

  Force F
    |               /|
    |             /  |
    |           /////|  <-- shaded area (triangle)
    |         ///////|      = ½ x F = elastic potential energy
    |       /////////|
    |     ///////////|
    |   /////////////|
    | ///////////////|
    +-----------------+------ Extension x
    0                 x

The energy stored equals the area under the force–extension graph.

8.5.3 Elastic Potential Energy of a Stretched Wire

For a wire of length L, cross-section A and Young modulus Y, the force needed to produce an extension l is F = (YA/L) l. The work done in stretching it from l = 0 to l = ΔL is

W = ∫0ΔL (YA/L) l dl = (YA/L) (ΔL)2/2

U = ½ (YA/L)(ΔL)2 = ½ F ΔL, where F = YAΔL/L is the final stretching force.

Equivalently, U = F2L / (2AY).

8.5.4 Elastic Energy per Unit Volume (Energy Density)

The volume of the wire is AL. Dividing U by AL:

u = U / (AL) = ½ (F/A)(ΔL/L)

u = ½ × stress × strain = ½ Y (strain)2 = (stress)2 / 2Y

The unit of u is J m−3. This is a very general result: the energy density stored in any elastic deformation is half the product of stress and strain.

Common Misconceptions

  • When a load Mg is slowly hung from a wire, the work done by gravity is MgΔL, but the energy stored in the wire is only ½ MgΔL. The other half is dissipated as heat and sound.
  • Elastic potential energy is proportional to the square of the extension, not the extension itself. Doubling the extension of a spring quadruples the stored energy.
  • For a given load, a thicker wire stores less elastic energy than a thin wire of the same length and material, since U = F2L/(2AY) ∝ 1/A.

Real-Life Applications

  • Bows, catapults, trampolines and pole-vault poles store elastic energy and release it as kinetic energy.
  • Wind-up toys and mechanical watches store energy in coiled springs.
  • Elastic energy stored in tendons contributes to efficient running in animals.
  • Shock absorbers convert elastic energy into heat through hysteresis and damping.

Worked Example 13 (Easy – Concept Clarity)

Problem: A spring of force constant 200 N m−1 is stretched by 5 cm. Find the elastic potential energy stored.

Solution:

U = ½ kx2 = ½ × 200 × (0.05)2 = 100 × 0.0025 = 0.25 J

Answer: U = 0.25 J

Worked Example 14 (Moderate – Practice Level)

Problem: A steel wire of length 2 m and cross-sectional area 1 mm2 is stretched by a load of 100 N. Find the elastic energy stored and the energy per unit volume. (Y = 2 × 1011 Pa)

Solution:

A = 1 mm2 = 10−6 m2

ΔL = FL/(AY) = (100 × 2)/(10−6 × 2 × 1011) = 200/(2 × 105) = 10−3 m

U = ½ F ΔL = ½ × 100 × 10−3 = 0.05 J

Volume = AL = 10−6 × 2 = 2 × 10−6 m3; u = 0.05/(2 × 10−6) = 2.5 × 104 J m−3

Check: stress = 100/10−6 = 108 Pa; strain = 10−3/2 = 5 × 10−4; ½ × stress × strain = ½ × 108 × 5 × 10−4 = 2.5 × 104 J m−3

Answer: U = 0.05 J; u = 2.5 × 104 J m−3

Worked Example 15 (Exam-Level – NEB Pattern)

Problem: A metal wire of length 3 m and cross-sectional area 1.5 mm2 is stretched by 3 mm. If Y = 2 × 1011 Pa, find (a) the tension in the wire, (b) the elastic energy stored, (c) the energy density.

Solution:

A = 1.5 × 10−6 m2; ΔL = 3 × 10−3 m.

(a) F = YAΔL/L = (2 × 1011 × 1.5 × 10−6 × 3 × 10−3)/3 = 300 N

(b) U = ½ FΔL = ½ × 300 × 3 × 10−3 = 0.45 J

(c) Volume = AL = 1.5 × 10−6 × 3 = 4.5 × 10−6 m3; u = 0.45/(4.5 × 10−6) = 1 × 105 J m−3

Check: strain = 3 × 10−3/3 = 10−3; ½ Y (strain)2 = ½ × 2 × 1011 × 10−6 = 1 × 105 J m−3

Answer: F = 300 N; U = 0.45 J; u = 1 × 105 J m−3

Exam Tips

  • Important formulas to memorise: F = kx; k = YA/L; springs: ks = k1k2/(k1 + k2), kp = k1 + k2; stress = F/A; strain = ΔL/L; Y = FL/AΔL; K = −ΔP/(ΔV/V); η = F/(Aθ); U = ½ kx2; u = ½ stress × strain.
  • Common board questions: (i) State Hooke's law and define force constant. (ii) Define stress and strain; distinguish the three types. (iii) Draw and explain the stress–strain curve. (iv) Derive relations among Y, K, η and σ.
  • Typical numerical patterns: (i) Given load, length, diameter: find stress, strain, extension. (ii) Two wires with different lengths, radii or materials under the same load: find the ratio of extensions. (iii) Energy stored in stretched springs or wires.
  • Always convert area from mm2 to m2 (1 mm2 = 10−6 m2) and lengths to metres before substituting. When only the diameter is given, calculate area using A = πd2/4.
  • Do not attach units to strain or Poisson's ratio.
  • In energy problems, use the factor ½. Forgetting it, and using FΔL, is the most frequent error.
  • In stress–strain diagrams, label the axes correctly (stress on the vertical axis, strain on the horizontal axis), mark all the key points, and state the physical meaning of each region.
