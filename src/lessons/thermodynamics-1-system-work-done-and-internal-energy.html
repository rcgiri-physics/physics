Introduction

Thermodynamics studies heat, work, temperature and the transformations of energy. This chapter develops the first law of thermodynamics, which is the energy-conservation principle applied to thermodynamic systems.

Thermodynamic system is a definite quantity of matter or a region of space selected for study. Everything outside it is the surroundings, and the real or imaginary surface separating them is the boundary.

The system and surroundings together make the universe. A system is described macroscopically by variables such as pressure P, volume V and absolute temperature T.

A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when its macroscopic properties do not change with time and there are no unbalanced flows within it. Thermal equilibrium requires uniform temperature, mechanical equilibrium requires no unbalanced pressure or force, and chemical equilibrium requires constant composition.

State and path functions. Pressure, volume, temperature, internal energy, enthalpy and entropy are state functions: their changes depend only on the initial and final states. Heat Q and work W are path functions: their values depend on the process used.

Work done during volume change

Consider gas behind a piston of cross-sectional area A. If its pressure is P, the force exerted by the gas is F = PA. When the piston moves through a small distance dx, the volume change is dV = A dx.

\[dW = F\,dx = PA\,dx = P\,dV\]

Therefore, the work done by the gas as its volume changes from V1 to V2 is

\[W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} P\,dV\]
Sign convention used here: work done by the system is positive. Thus an expansion has W > 0, while compression has W < 0. Some books define work done on the system as positive; always state the convention before solving.

Work at constant pressure

\[W = P(V_2-V_1)=P\Delta V\]

Work from a P–V diagram

On a pressure–volume diagram, the work done is the signed area under the process curve between the initial and final volumes. Expansion to the right gives positive work and compression to the left gives negative work. In a cyclic process, the net work is the area enclosed by the cycle: clockwise cycles produce work, while counter-clockwise cycles require work input.

Isothermal process

For an ideal gas at constant temperature, \(PV=nRT\), so \(P=nRT/V\). Hence

\[W=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{nRT}{V}\,dV=nRT\ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)\]

For an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature. Therefore \(\Delta U=0\) in an isothermal process.

Adiabatic process

An adiabatic process occurs without heat exchange with the surroundings. For a reversible adiabatic change of an ideal gas,

\[PV^\gamma=\text{constant},\qquad TV^{\gamma-1}=\text{constant}\]

Expansion causes cooling and compression causes heating. Here \(\gamma=C_P/C_V\).

Heat and internal energy

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference; it is not a substance stored in a body. Internal energy U is the total microscopic energy of the system, including molecular kinetic energy and intermolecular potential energy.

\[U=U_{\text{kinetic}}+U_{\text{potential}}\]

For an ideal gas, intermolecular potential energy is negligible, so internal energy is a function of temperature only. For a monatomic ideal gas,

\[U=\frac{3}{2}nRT,\qquad \Delta U=nC_V\Delta T\]
Heat capacity. The heat capacity of a body is the heat required to raise its temperature by one kelvin. The molar heat capacities are \(C_V\) at constant volume and \(C_P\) at constant pressure.

First law of thermodynamics

First law: The heat supplied to a system is used partly to increase its internal energy and partly to do work by the system.
\[\boxed{Q=\Delta U+W}\]

With this convention, \(Q>0\) means heat enters the system, \(Q<0\) means heat leaves it, \(W>0\) means the system does work, and \(W<0\) means work is done on it.

Special cases

  • Isochoric: \(W=0\), so \(Q=\Delta U\).
  • Adiabatic: \(Q=0\), so \(\Delta U=-W\).
  • Isothermal ideal gas: \(\Delta U=0\), so \(Q=W\).
  • Cyclic process: \(\Delta U=0\), so net heat supplied equals net work done.

Heat capacities and Mayer's relation

For one mole of an ideal gas, the first law gives

\[dQ=dU+P\,dV\]

At constant volume, \(dV=0\), hence \(dQ_V=C_VdT\). At constant pressure, \(dQ_P=C_PdT\). For one mole, using \(PV=RT\),

\[C_P-C_V=R\]

This is Mayer's relation. For an ideal gas, \(\gamma=C_P/C_V\), so \(C_P=\gamma R/(\gamma-1)\) and \(C_V=R/(\gamma-1)\).

Worked examples

1. Constant-pressure expansion. A gas expands from \(2.0\times10^{-3}\,\text{m}^3\) to \(5.0\times10^{-3}\,\text{m}^3\) against a constant pressure of \(1.0\times10^5\,\text{Pa}\).

\[W=P\Delta V=(1.0\times10^5)(3.0\times10^{-3})=3.0\times10^2\,\text{J}\]
The work done by the gas is 300 J.
2. First-law calculation. A system receives 500 J of heat and does 180 J of work. Find its change in internal energy.

\[\Delta U=Q-W=500-180=320\,\text{J}\]
The internal energy increases by 320 J.
3. Adiabatic compression. In an adiabatic compression, 250 J of work is done on the gas. Since work done by the gas is \(W=-250\,\text{J}\) and \(Q=0\),

\[\Delta U=Q-W=0-(-250)=250\,\text{J}\]
The gas gains 250 J of internal energy and its temperature rises.

Key points and common mistakes

  • Heat and work are transfers of energy, not state properties stored in a system.
  • Internal energy is a state function; heat and work depend on the path.
  • Use absolute temperature in gas laws and logarithmic isothermal-work formulas.
  • Work is the signed area under a P–V curve, not simply pressure times volume in every process.
  • At constant volume, no boundary work is done.
  • Do not use \(Q=mc\Delta T\) for a phase change or a general thermodynamic process without checking the assumptions.
  • State your sign convention. The equation changes appearance if a textbook defines work done on the system as positive.

  4. Show that the area under a P–V curve represents work done.

  8. Derive Mayer's relation \(C_P-C_V=R\) for one mole of an ideal gas.

Summary

  • \(dW=P\,dV\) and \(W=\int P\,dV\).
  • First law: \(Q=\Delta U+W\).
  • Isobaric: \(W=P\Delta V\); isochoric: \(W=0\); adiabatic: \(Q=0\).
  • For an ideal gas, \(\Delta U=nC_V\Delta T\), and \(C_P-C_V=R\).
  • For a reversible adiabatic process, \(PV^\gamma=\text{constant}\).
