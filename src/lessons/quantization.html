23.1 Postulates of Bohr’s Atomic Model

To resolve the classical instability of Rutherford's nuclear atom—where continuously accelerating electrons ought to radiate energy and collapse into the nucleus—Niels Bohr (1913) incorporated Planck's quantum concept into atomic structure through three fundamental postulates:

  1. Postulate of Stationary Orbits (Mechanical Equilibrium): An electron moves around the positively charged nucleus in certain stable, non-radiating circular orbits known as stationary orbits. While revolving in these orbits, the necessary centripetal force is provided entirely by the electrostatic Coulomb attraction between the nucleus ($+Ze$) and the electron ($-e$). $$\frac{m_e v^2}{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Z e^2}{r^2}$$ For Hydrogen ($Z = 1$): $$\frac{m_e v^2}{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{r^2}$$
  2. Postulate of Angular Momentum Quantization: Out of an infinite number of classically possible orbits, an electron can revolve only in those discrete orbits where its orbital angular momentum ($L$) is an integral multiple of $\frac{h}{2\pi}$ (where $h$ is Planck's constant). $$L = m_e v r = \frac{n h}{2\pi} \quad \text{where } n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$ Here, $n$ is called the principal quantum number.
  3. Postulate of Quantum Transition (Frequency Condition): An electron radiates or absorbs energy only when it jumps from one non-radiating stationary orbit to another. Radiation of frequency $\nu$ is emitted when an electron makes a transition from an orbit of higher energy $E_2$ to an orbit of lower energy $E_1$: $$\Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = h \nu = \frac{h c}{\lambda}$$

23.2 Mathematical Derivations for Hydrogen Atom

A. Expression for Radius of $n$-th Orbit ($r_n$)

From Bohr's second postulate, the electron velocity in the $n$-th orbit is:

$$v_n = \frac{n h}{2\pi m_e r_n} \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$

Substituting Equation (1) into Bohr's first postulate ($\frac{m_e v_n^2}{r_n} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{r_n^2}$):

$$\frac{m_e}{r_n} \left( \frac{n h}{2\pi m_e r_n} \right)^2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{r_n^2}$$ $$\frac{m_e n^2 h^2}{4\pi^2 m_e^2 r_n^3} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_n^2}$$

Solving directly for $r_n$:

Radius of $n$-th Orbit
$$r_n = \frac{n^2 h^2 \epsilon_0}{\pi m_e e^2}$$

Since $h, \epsilon_0, m_e,$ and $e$ are fundamental physical constants, $r_n \propto n^2$. For $n = 1$ (the ground state Bohr radius $r_1$):

$$r_1 = \frac{(1)^2 \times (6.626 \times 10^{-34})^2 \times (8.854 \times 10^{-12})}{\pi \times (9.109 \times 10^{-31}) \times (1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2} \approx 5.29 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m} = 0.529 \text{ \AA}$$

B. Expression for Velocity of Electron in $n$-th Orbit ($v_n$)

Substituting $r_n = \frac{n^2 h^2 \epsilon_0}{\pi m_e e^2}$ into Equation (1):

$$v_n = \frac{n h}{2\pi m_e \left(\frac{n^2 h^2 \epsilon_0}{\pi m_e e^2}\right)}$$
Orbital Velocity
$$v_n = \frac{e^2}{2 \epsilon_0 n h}$$

Note that $v_n \propto \frac{1}{n}$. For the ground state ($n = 1$):

$$v_1 = \frac{(1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2}{2 \times (8.854 \times 10^{-12}) \times 1 \times (6.626 \times 10^{-34})} \approx 2.18 \times 10^6 \text{ m/s}$$

This is approximately $\frac{1}{137}$ times the speed of light ($c$).

C. Expressions for Kinetic, Potential, and Total Energy

  1. Kinetic Energy ($K_n$): $$K_n = \frac{1}{2} m_e v_n^2 = \frac{1}{2} m_e \left( \frac{e^2}{2\epsilon_0 n h} \right)^2 = \frac{m_e e^4}{8 \epsilon_0^2 n^2 h^2}$$
  2. Potential Energy ($U_n$): Due to electrostatic interaction with the nucleus at distance $r_n$: $$U_n = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{r_n} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{\left(\frac{n^2 h^2 \epsilon_0}{\pi m_e e^2}\right)} = -\frac{m_e e^4}{4 \epsilon_0^2 n^2 h^2}$$
  3. Total Energy ($E_n$): Sum of kinetic and potential energies: $$E_n = K_n + U_n = \frac{m_e e^4}{8 \epsilon_0^2 n^2 h^2} - \frac{m_e e^4}{4 \epsilon_0^2 n^2 h^2} = -\frac{m_e e^4}{8 \epsilon_0^2 n^2 h^2}$$
Total Energy in Electron-Volts (eV)
$$E_n = -\frac{m_e e^4}{8 \epsilon_0^2 n^2 h^2} = -\frac{13.6}{n^2} \text{ eV}$$

Physical Significance of the Negative Sign: The negative total energy signifies that the electron is electrostatically bound to the nucleus. External work must be performed on the system to remove the electron to infinity ($E_\infty = 0$).

23.3 Wavelength of Emitted Spectral Line

When an electron transitions from an initial higher energy orbit $n_2$ to a final lower energy orbit $n_1$ ($n_2 > n_1$), the emitted photon energy is:

$$h \nu = E_{n2} - E_{n1}$$ $$\frac{h c}{\lambda} = \left( -\frac{m_e e^4}{8 \epsilon_0^2 n_2^2 h^2} \right) - \left( -\frac{m_e e^4}{8 \epsilon_0^2 n_1^2 h^2} \right)$$ $$\frac{h c}{\lambda} = \frac{m_e e^4}{8 \epsilon_0^2 h^2} \left[ \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right]$$

Dividing both sides by $h c$ yields the wave number ($\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda}$):

Rydberg Formula for Spectral Lines
$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R \left[ \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right]$$ $$\text{where } R = \frac{m_e e^4}{8 \epsilon_0^2 h^3 c} \approx 1.097 \times 10^7 \text{ m}^{-1} \quad \text{(Rydberg Constant)}$$

23.5 Excitation Potential vs Ionization Potential

Excitation Energy: The minimum energy required to transition an electron from its ground state ($n=1$) to any higher bound excited state ($n > 1$).

Excitation Potential: The accelerating potential difference through which an electron must pass to gain energy equal to the excitation energy.

$$V_{excitation} = \frac{E_n - E_1}{e}$$

Ionization Energy: The minimum energy required to completely remove an electron from its ground state to infinity ($n = \infty$).

Ionization Potential: The accelerating potential difference required to impart ionization energy to the bound electron.

$$V_{ionization} = \frac{E_\infty - E_1}{e} = \frac{0 - (-13.6 \text{ eV})}{e} = 13.6 \text{ Volts}$$
Parameter Excitation Potential Ionization Potential
Definition Potential required to shift an electron from ground state to an excited state ($n > 1$). Potential required to completely detach the electron from the atom ($n = \infty$).
Final State Bound higher energy state (e.g., $n=2, 3, 4$). Unbound free state ($n = \infty$, $E = 0$).
Value for Hydrogen First Excitation Potential = $10.2 \text{ V}$ ($n=1 \to 2$). Ionization Potential = $13.6 \text{ V}$.

23.6 Emission and Absorption Spectra

A. Emission Spectrum

When atoms or molecules are excited (by thermal heating, electric discharge, or radiation), electrons absorb energy and jump to higher energy levels. Upon returning to lower levels within $\sim 10^{-8} \text{ s}$, they emit light of specific frequencies. This produces an emission spectrum consisting of bright colored lines on a dark background.

  • Continuous Spectrum: Produced by incandescent solids, liquids, or dense gases (e.g., tungsten filament lamp). Contains a continuous band of all wavelengths.
  • Line Spectrum: Produced by excited atoms in monoatomic gaseous states (e.g., Sodium or Mercury vapor lamp). Contains sharp, distinct bright lines characteristic of the element.
  • Band Spectrum: Produced by excited molecular gases (e.g., Nitrogen gas). Consists of closely spaced groups of lines forming bands with sharp heads.

B. Absorption Spectrum

When continuous white light passes through a cooler gas or vapor, the gas atoms absorb precisely those specific wavelengths that they are capable of emitting when excited. The resulting spectrum consists of dark absorption lines appearing against a continuous bright rainbow background.

Fraunhofer Lines: Dark absorption lines observed in the solar spectrum caused by selective absorption of continuous light from the hot solar photosphere as it passes through cooler gases in the solar atmosphere (chromosphere).

23.7 De Broglie Hypothesis & Matter Waves

In 1924, Louis de Broglie proposed that nature exhibits symmetry. Since radiation possesses dual nature (exhibiting both wave behavior in interference/diffraction and particle behavior in photoelectric effect), material particles in motion (electrons, protons, atoms) must also exhibit wave properties.

De Broglie Wavelength Equation
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{m_e v}$$

De Broglie Wavelength of an Accelerated Electron

When an electron of mass $m_e$ and charge $e$ is accelerated from rest through a potential difference $V$, its gained kinetic energy is:

$$K = e V = \frac{1}{2} m_e v^2 = \frac{p^2}{2 m_e} \implies p = \sqrt{2 m_e e V}$$

Substituting momentum $p$ into de Broglie's equation:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m_e e V}}$$

Inserting standard physical values ($h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}$, $m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}$, $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$):

Practical Electron Wavelength Formula
$$\lambda = \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{V}} \text{ \AA} = \frac{1.227}{\sqrt{V}} \text{ nm}$$

Explanation of Bohr's Quantization Condition

De Broglie explained Bohr’s second postulate by asserting that an electron forms a stable standing wave along its circular orbit. For constructive interference, the circumference of the orbit must contain an integral number of de Broglie wavelengths ($n\lambda$):

$$2 \pi r = n \lambda = n \left( \frac{h}{m_e v} \right)$$ $$m_e v r = \frac{n h}{2\pi}$$

Thus, Bohr's angular momentum quantization condition arises directly from the wave nature of electrons!

23.8 Definition of X-Rays

X-rays are highly energetic, short-wavelength electromagnetic radiations ranging from approximately $0.01 \text{ nm}$ to $10 \text{ nm}$ ($0.1 \text{ \AA}$ to $100 \text{ \AA}$). They were discovered by Wilhelm Röntgen in 1895.

Production Principle: X-rays are produced when fast-moving electrons accelerated through high electric potentials suddenly decelerate or collide with a heavy target metal of high atomic number and high melting point.

23.9 Modern Coolidge Tube Method for Production of X-Rays

The modern Coolidge tube is a hot-cathode evacuated glass tube that allows independent control over the quality (penetrating power) and quantity (intensity) of produced X-rays.

Construction & Working Mechanism

  • Filament Cathode ($F$): A tungsten filament heated by a low-tension battery ($LT$) emits electrons continuously via thermionic emission.
  • Anode Target ($T$): A high melting point metal (such as Tungsten or Molybdenum) embedded in a heavy copper block to rapidly conduct away vast amounts of heat.
  • High Tension Supply ($HT$): A potential difference of $20 \text{ kV}$ to $100 \text{ kV}$ accelerates thermionic electrons toward the anode target.
  • Cooling System: Water or cooling fins attached to the copper anode dissipate thermal energy, as over $98\%$ of electron kinetic energy converts to heat and under $2\%$ into X-rays.

Figure 23.4: Line schematic of the modern Coolidge X-Ray Tube detailing electron acceleration and copper anode cooling system.

Control of Quality and Quantity

  1. Control of Quality (Hardness / Penetrating Power): Quality depends on the kinetic energy of striking electrons, which is governed directly by the accelerating High Voltage ($V$). Increasing $V$ decreases the minimum cutoff wavelength ($\lambda_{\min}$), yielding high-frequency, highly penetrating Hard X-rays.
  2. Control of Quantity (Intensity / Number of Photons): Quantity depends on the number of electrons hitting the target per second, which is controlled by adjusting the filament heating current ($I_f$). Increasing $I_f$ increases thermionic emission rate, resulting in higher X-ray intensity without altering penetrating power.

Continuous Spectrum & Duane-Hunt Law

When an electron is completely stopped in a single collision with a target nucleus, its entire kinetic energy converts into a single X-ray photon of maximum frequency $\nu_{\max}$ and minimum wavelength $\lambda_{\min}$:

$$E_{k} = e V = h \nu_{\max} = \frac{h c}{\lambda_{\min}}$$
Duane-Hunt Law Formula
$$\lambda_{\min} = \frac{h c}{e V} = \frac{12400}{V} \text{ \AA}$$

23.10 Properties and Applications of X-Rays

Key Physical Properties

  • They are neutral electromagnetic waves and remain undeflected in electric or magnetic fields.
  • They travel in straight lines in vacuum at the speed of light ($c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$).
  • They possess strong ionizing power when passing through gases.
  • They penetrate opaque substances such as wood, flesh, thin metal sheets, and plastics.
  • They cause fluorescence in materials like zinc sulfide ($\text{ZnS}$) and barium platinocyanide.
  • They exhibit wave properties including diffraction by atomic planes in crystals (governed by Bragg's Law: $2d \sin\theta = n\lambda$).

Comparison: Hard X-Rays vs Soft X-Rays

Property Hard X-Rays Soft X-Rays
Wavelength ($\lambda$) Shorter ($0.01 \text{ \AA} - 1 \text{ \AA}$) Longer ($1 \text{ \AA} - 100 \text{ \AA}$)
Frequency ($\nu$) High frequency / high energy Lower frequency / lower energy
Accelerating Voltage Produced at high voltages ($> 40 \text{ kV}$) Produced at lower voltages ($< 10 \text{ kV}$)
Penetrating Power High penetrating power Low penetrating power (absorbed easily)
Primary Application Industrial flaw detection, bone radiography Soft tissue inspection, dermatology

Applications of X-Rays

  1. Medical Diagnostics & Radiotherapy: Detection of bone fractures, dental cavities, foreign metal bodies, CT scanning, and localized destruction of cancerous tumor cells.
  2. Industrial Inspection: Inspection of internal casting cracks, structural weld defects, and quality control of manufactured alloys.
  3. Scientific Research: X-ray crystallography for determining 3D molecular structures of biological proteins and crystal lattice spacings.

23.11 Step-by-Step NEB Solved Numerical Examples

Example 1: Wavelength of $H_\alpha$ Line in Balmer Series

Problem: Calculate the wavelength of the first line ($H_\alpha$) and the series limit of the Balmer series for hydrogen atom. ($R = 1.097 \times 10^7 \text{ m}^{-1}$)

Given: For Balmer series, $n_1 = 2$.

1. For First Line ($H_\alpha$): $n_2 = 3$.

$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R \left[ \frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{3^2} \right] = 1.097 \times 10^7 \left[ \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{9} \right] = 1.097 \times 10^7 \times \frac{5}{36} = 1.5236 \times 10^6 \text{ m}^{-1}$$ $$\lambda = \frac{1}{1.5236 \times 10^6} = 6.563 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m} = 656.3 \text{ nm}$$

2. For Series Limit (Shortest Wavelength): $n_2 = \infty$.

$$\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}} = R \left[ \frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{\infty^2} \right] = \frac{R}{4}$$ $$\lambda_{\min} = \frac{4}{1.097 \times 10^7} = 3.646 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m} = 364.6 \text{ nm}$$
Example 2: Radius and Velocity in Second Orbit

Problem: Calculate the radius and electron velocity in the second stationary orbit ($n=2$) of hydrogen atom.

Formulae: $r_n = n^2 r_1$ where $r_1 = 0.529 \text{ \AA}$, and $v_n = \frac{v_1}{n}$ where $v_1 = 2.18 \times 10^6 \text{ m/s}$.

$$\text{Radius } r_2 = (2)^2 \times 0.529 \text{ \AA} = 2.116 \text{ \AA} = 2.116 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m}$$ $$\text{Velocity } v_2 = \frac{2.18 \times 10^6}{2} = 1.09 \times 10^6 \text{ m/s}$$
Example 3: De Broglie Wavelength of Accelerated Electron

Problem: An electron is accelerated through a potential difference of $100 \text{ V}$. Find its de Broglie wavelength.

Using Formula: $\lambda = \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{V}} \text{ \AA}$

$$\lambda = \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{100}} = \frac{12.27}{10} = 1.227 \text{ \AA} = 0.1227 \text{ nm}$$
Example 4: Duane-Hunt Cutoff Wavelength for X-Ray Tube

Problem: Calculate the minimum wavelength of X-rays produced by a Coolidge tube operating at $50 \text{ kV}$.

Given: $V = 50 \text{ kV} = 50,000 \text{ V}$.

$$\lambda_{\min} = \frac{h c}{e V} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 3 \times 10^8}{1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 50000} = \frac{1.9878 \times 10^{-25}}{8.01 \times 10^{-15}} = 2.48 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m} = 0.248 \text{ \AA}$$
