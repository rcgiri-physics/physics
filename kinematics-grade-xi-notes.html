🚀 Kinematics — Motion in a Straight Line and Plane

Grade XI Physics - NEB Curriculum

Unit 3: Kinematics

Kinematics is the branch of mechanics that describes motion — position, velocity and acceleration — without asking what causes it. This chapter develops instantaneous velocity and acceleration from first principles, treats relative motion, and applies the equations of uniformly accelerated motion to projectiles and falling bodies.

3.1 Instantaneous Velocity and Acceleration

3.1(a) Average Velocity

Displacement is a vector: it depends only on the initial and final positions. The average velocity over a time interval Δt is:

v_av = Δx / Δt = (x₂ − x₁) / (t₂ − t₁)

Average velocity hides detail, because a body may speed up and slow down within the interval. To describe motion at a single instant, the interval is made smaller and smaller.

3.1(b) Instantaneous Velocity

The instantaneous velocity is the limit of the average velocity as Δt approaches zero:

v = lim (Δt → 0) Δx / Δt = dx / dt

Figure 3.1: Chord gives average velocity; tangent at P gives instantaneous velocity

Speed is the magnitude of instantaneous velocity. Average speed = total distance / total time, which is larger than the magnitude of average velocity whenever the path is not a straight line.

3.1(c) Acceleration

Average acceleration is a_av = Δv / Δt. The instantaneous acceleration is:

a = dv / dt = d²x / dt²

Its SI unit is m s⁻² and its dimensions are [LT⁻²]. If a and v have the same sign the body speeds up; if opposite, it slows down (retardation).

Example 3.1

A particle moves as x = t³ − 6t² + 9t (SI units). Find when it is momentarily at rest and its acceleration at those instants.

Solution:

v = dx/dt = 3t² − 12t + 9 = 3(t − 1)(t − 3) = 0
⇒ t = 1 s and t = 3 s
a = dv/dt = 6t − 12
At t = 1 s: a = −6 m s⁻²
At t = 3 s: a = +6 m s⁻²

3.2 Relative Velocity

Velocity is always measured relative to an observer (frame of reference). The velocity of A relative to B is:

v_AB = v_A − v_B (both measured from the ground)

One-dimensional cases:

  • Same direction: relative speed = |v_A − v_B|
  • Opposite directions: relative speed = v_A + v_B
  • v_BA = − v_AB

3.2(a) River–Boat Problems

Let v_r be the river speed, v_b the boat speed in still water and d the river width. The velocity of the boat relative to the ground is the vector sum v_bg = v_b + v_r.

(a) Shortest time:
Boat heads perpendicular to bank: t = d / v_b
Drift downstream = v_r × t

(b) Shortest path (zero drift):
sin θ = v_r / v_b (θ from the perpendicular, upstream)
Effective speed = √(v_b² − v_r²)
t = d / √(v_b² − v_r²) (needs v_b > v_r)

3.2(b) Rain and the Moving Man

Rain falls vertically at v_R while a man walks at v_M. The rain relative to the man is v_RM = v_R − v_M:

|v_RM| = √(v_R² + v_M²)
tan θ = v_M / v_R (θ from the vertical, slanting towards the man)

He must tilt his umbrella forward by angle θ to stay dry.

Example 3.2

A river 200 m wide flows at 3 m s⁻¹. A boat has speed 5 m s⁻¹ in still water. Find the time and drift for (a) shortest time, (b) shortest path.

Solution:

(a) t = 200 / 5 = 40 s
Drift = 3 × 40 = 120 m

(b) sin θ = 3/5, so θ ≈ 37° upstream
Effective speed = √(25 − 9) = 4 m s⁻¹
t = 200 / 4 = 50 s, drift = 0

Example 3.3

A man walks at 4 km h⁻¹ in rain falling vertically at 3 km h⁻¹. Find the apparent velocity of rain.

|v_RM| = √(4² + 3²) = 5 km h⁻¹
θ = tan⁻¹(4/3) ≈ 53° from the vertical

For motion in a straight line with uniform acceleration, let u = initial velocity, v = final velocity after time t, a = acceleration and s = displacement.

Figure 3.3: Velocity–time graph for uniform acceleration

Derivation 1: v = u + at

Slope of the v–t graph is the acceleration:

a = (v − u) / t
v = u + at

Derivation 2: s = ut + ½at²

Displacement is the area under the graph: a rectangle (u × t) plus a triangle (½ × t × (v − u)):

s = ut + ½ × t × (at)
s = ut + ½at²

Derivation 3: v² = u² + 2as

Area of the trapezoid gives s = ½(u + v)t. Substituting t = (v − u)/a:

s = (v + u)(v − u) / 2a = (v² − u²) / 2a
v² = u² + 2as

Distance travelled in the nth second:

s_n = u + ½a(2n − 1)

⚠️ Important:

These equations hold only for constant acceleration. Choose a positive direction first and give every vector (u, v, a, s) its correct sign.

Example 3.4

A car moving at 20 m s⁻¹ brakes with retardation 5 m s⁻². Find the stopping distance and time.

0 = 20² + 2(−5)s ⇒ s = 40 m
0 = 20 − 5t ⇒ t = 4 s

Example 3.5

A body starts with u = 2 m s⁻¹ and a = 4 m s⁻². Find the distance in the 3rd second.

s₃ = 2 + ½(4)(2×3 − 1) = 2 + 10 = 12 m

3.4 Motion of a Freely Falling Body

A body moving under gravity alone (air resistance neglected) is in free fall, whether it is dropped, thrown up or thrown down. All bodies have the same acceleration g ≈ 9.8 m s⁻² toward Earth's centre.

     Thrown upward                    Dropped from height h
     (up = positive, a = −g)          (down = positive, a = +g)

        ● top: v = 0                        ● u = 0
        ↑                                   │  ↓
        │  H                                │  h    v = gt
        │                                   │  ↓
        │                                   ▼
        ● u  (launch)                     ─────── ground
     ═══════════ ground
Figure 3.4: Upward throw and free drop, with sign conventions

3.4(a) Body Dropped from Height h (u = 0)

v = gt
h = ½gt²
v² = 2gh
t = √(2h / g)

3.4(b) Body Thrown Vertically Upward with Speed u

v = u − gt
y = ut − ½gt²
v² = u² − 2gy

Time to rise: t_up = u / g
Maximum height: H = u² / 2g
Total time of flight: T = 2u / g

Key features:

  • At the highest point v = 0, but the acceleration is still g downward.
  • Time of ascent = time of descent.
  • The body passes any height with equal speed going up and coming down.
  • It returns to the starting point with speed u.

Example 3.6

A ball is thrown upward at 20 m s⁻¹ (g = 9.8 m s⁻²). Find the maximum height and total time of flight.

H = 20² / (2 × 9.8) = 400 / 19.6 ≈ 20.4 m
t_up = 20 / 9.8 ≈ 2.04 s
T = 2 × 2.04 ≈ 4.08 s

Example 3.7

A stone is dropped from a height of 19.6 m. Find the time taken and the impact speed.

t = √(2 × 19.6 / 9.8) = 2 s
v = 9.8 × 2 = 19.6 m s⁻¹

3.5 Projectile Motion and Its Applications

A projectile is a body thrown into the air that then moves under gravity alone. The key idea is that horizontal and vertical motions are independent. Horizontally there is no acceleration; vertically there is constant acceleration g downward.

3.5(a) Projectile Fired at Angle θ from Level Ground

   y
   ↑                  · · · ·  ← highest point : v = u cosθ (→)
   │              ·               ·
   │           ·      ¦              ·
   │         ·        ¦ H              ·
   │       ·          ¦                  ·
   │  u  ╱ ·          ¦                    ·
   │   ╱ θ            ¦                      ·
   └──────────────────────────────────────────→ x
   O   u cosθ (horizontal),  u sinθ (vertical)
   ├──────────────── Range R ────────────────┤
Figure 3.5: Trajectory of a projectile launched at angle θ

Components of the initial velocity: u_x = u cosθ and u_y = u sinθ. At time t:

x = (u cosθ) t
y = (u sinθ) t − ½gt²
v_x = u cosθ
v_y = u sinθ − gt

Equation of trajectory

Eliminate t using t = x / (u cosθ):

y = x tanθ − g x² / (2u² cos²θ)

This has the form y = bx − cx², which is a parabola.

Time of flight, maximum height and range

Time of flight (return to y = 0):
0 = (u sinθ)T − ½gT² ⇒ T = 2u sinθ / g

Maximum height (v_y = 0 at t = u sinθ / g):
H = u² sin²θ / 2g

Horizontal range:
R = (u cosθ) T = 2u² sinθ cosθ / g
R = u² sin2θ / g

Consequences:

  • Range is maximum, R_max = u²/g, when θ = 45°.
  • Angles θ and (90° − θ) give the same range, since sin2θ = sin(180° − 2θ).
  • At the highest point the speed is u cosθ (horizontal); the vertical velocity is zero.
  • On level ground the landing speed equals u, at angle θ below the horizontal.
  • At 45°, R = 4H.

3.5(b) Horizontal Projection from a Height h

        u
     ●─────→ · ·
     │            ·
     │              ·
     │  h              ·
     │                   ·
     │                     ·  ↓ v
   ══╧════════════════════════ ground
     ├───────── Range R ──────┤
Figure 3.6: Horizontal projection — initial vertical velocity is zero
Time of fall: t = √(2h / g)
Horizontal range: R = u √(2h / g)
Vertical velocity at landing: v_y = gt
Landing speed: v = √(u² + g²t²)

The time of fall does not depend on u. A bullet fired horizontally and another dropped from the same height reach level ground together.

3.5(c) Applications

  • Sports: football kick, javelin, cricket shot, basketball throw.
  • Water jets: hoses and fountains follow parabolic paths.
  • Aircraft: dropping relief supplies or bombs from a moving plane.
  • Artillery: range and angle calculations.

In real situations air resistance shortens range and height and makes the path asymmetric.

Example 3.8

A ball is thrown at 20 m s⁻¹ at 30° above the horizontal (g = 9.8 m s⁻²). Find T, H and R.

T = 2 × 20 × 0.5 / 9.8 ≈ 2.04 s
H = 400 × 0.25 / 19.6 ≈ 5.1 m
R = 400 × 0.866 / 9.8 ≈ 35.3 m

Example 3.9

A stone is thrown horizontally at 15 m s⁻¹ from a cliff 78.4 m high. Find the range and landing speed.

t = √(2 × 78.4 / 9.8) = 4 s
R = 15 × 4 = 60 m
v_y = 9.8 × 4 = 39.2 m s⁻¹
v = √(15² + 39.2²) = √1761.6 ≈ 42 m s⁻¹

Key Formulas and Summary

Fundamental Relations:

v = dx/dt , a = dv/dt
v_AB = v_A − v_B
River: t_min = d/v_b ; sinθ = v_r/v_b (zero drift)

Equations of Motion (constant a):

v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as
s_n = u + ½a(2n − 1)

Free Fall and Projectile:

Free fall (up): H = u²/2g , T = 2u/g
Drop: t = √(2h/g) , v = √(2gh)
Projectile: T = 2u sinθ/g , H = u² sin²θ/2g , R = u² sin2θ/g
Trajectory: y = x tanθ − gx²/(2u² cos²θ)

Practice Numerical Problems

Take g = 9.8 m s⁻² unless stated otherwise. Attempt each problem before reading the answer.

1. A particle has x = 3t² + 2t + 1 (SI). Find its velocity and acceleration at t = 2 s.

Answer: v = 6t + 2 = 14 m s⁻¹; a = 6 m s⁻².

2. Car A moves east at 60 km h⁻¹ and car B west at 40 km h⁻¹. Find v_AB. Then find v_AB if B also moves east at 40 km h⁻¹.

Answer: 100 km h⁻¹ east; then 20 km h⁻¹ east.

3. A swimmer (4 m s⁻¹ in still water) crosses a 120 m wide river flowing at 3 m s⁻¹. Find the minimum time and drift, and the time to land directly opposite.

Answer: t_min = 120/4 = 30 s, drift = 90 m. Directly opposite: speed = √(16 − 9) = √7 ≈ 2.65 m s⁻¹, so t ≈ 45.3 s.

4. A train at 72 km h⁻¹ brakes and stops in 50 m. Find the retardation and stopping time.

Answer: u = 20 m s⁻¹; 0 = 400 + 2a(50) ⇒ a = −4 m s⁻²; t = 5 s.

5. A body starts from rest with a = 2 m s⁻². Find its displacement in the 5th second.

Answer: s₅ = 0 + ½(2)(9) = 9 m.

6. A stone falls from a tower and reaches the ground in 3 s. Find the height and impact speed.

Answer: h = ½(9.8)(9) = 44.1 m; v = 29.4 m s⁻¹.

7. A ball is thrown upward at 29.4 m s⁻¹. Find the maximum height, the time to return and its velocity after 4 s.

Answer: H = 44.1 m; T = 6 s; v = 29.4 − 39.2 = −9.8 m s⁻¹ (9.8 m s⁻¹ downward).

8. A projectile is launched at 40 m s⁻¹ at 45°. Find T, H and R.

Answer: T ≈ 5.77 s; H = 1600(0.5)/19.6 ≈ 40.8 m; R = 1600/9.8 ≈ 163.3 m.

9. What launch speed is needed for a range of 100 m at 45°?

Answer: u = √(Rg) = √980 ≈ 31.3 m s⁻¹.

10. An aeroplane flying horizontally at 100 m s⁻¹ at a height of 490 m releases a packet. How far ahead horizontally does it land?

Answer: t = √(2 × 490/9.8) = 10 s; R = 100 × 10 = 1000 m.

Conceptual and Long Answer Questions

1. Can a body have zero velocity and non-zero acceleration? Give an example.
Yes. A ball at the top of its flight has v = 0 but a = g downward.
2. Why is the path of a projectile a parabola?
x grows linearly with t and y quadratically; eliminating t gives y = bx − cx².
3. A bullet is fired horizontally and another dropped from the same height at the same instant. Which lands first?
Both together, since vertical motion is independent of horizontal velocity.
4. Distinguish between average speed and the magnitude of average velocity.
Speed uses total path length; velocity uses net displacement. They are equal only for straight-line motion without reversal.
5. Derive the three equations of motion using the velocity–time graph. (Section 3.3)
6. Derive expressions for T, H and R of a projectile and show that its trajectory is a parabola. (Section 3.5)
